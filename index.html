Deep Time

Origins of Life

From the formation of Earth 4.54 billion years ago to the last woolly mammoth on Wrangel Island -- a complete timeline of every major chapter in the history of life on this planet.

4.54 Ga
Earth Forms
Accretion from the solar nebula. A molten, crater-scarred world with a reducing atmosphere of methane, ammonia, and water vapour. No oxygen. No life. The Moon forms from a giant impact (Theia hypothesis) within the first 100 million years.
~4.1 Ga
Late Heavy Bombardment
A period of intense asteroid and comet impacts. Some scientists propose these impacts may have delivered organic molecules and water to early Earth. Others argue life could not have survived this period at surface level.
~3.8 Ga
Earliest Life Traces
Carbon isotope signatures in 3.8-billion-year-old Greenland rocks (Isua formation) suggest biological activity. Stromatolites at Warrawoona, Western Australia (~3.5 Ga) are the oldest confirmed fossils -- layered mats of cyanobacteria, still forming in Shark Bay today.
~3.5 Ga
Prokaryotes Diversify
Single-celled organisms without a nucleus dominate all life. Bacteria and archaea fill every available niche -- hydrothermal vents, acidic hot springs, hypersaline lagoons. This microbial world will remain the dominant form of life for the next 3 billion years.
~2.7 Ga
Cyanobacteria Photosynthesis
Cyanobacteria evolve oxygenic photosynthesis -- splitting water molecules to release free oxygen. A world-changing biological innovation. For ~300 million years, the oxygen released is absorbed by iron in the oceans, rusting them and creating the banded iron formations we mine today.
~2.4 Ga
Great Oxidation Event
The iron-oxygen buffer saturates. Free oxygen floods the atmosphere for the first time. For most anaerobic life this is a catastrophe -- the first mass extinction. For aerobic organisms it is the beginning of everything. Atmospheric O2 climbs from near zero to ~2%.
~1.8 Ga
First Eukaryotes
Cells with a true nucleus appear, likely from endosymbiosis -- an archaeon absorbing a bacterium and keeping it as a mitochondrion. The last common ancestor of all eukaryotes alive today traces here. This is the evolutionary step that eventually makes complex multicellular life possible.
~1.2 Ga
Sexual Reproduction Evolves
The mixing of genetic material from two parents dramatically accelerates evolution. Variation, not just mutation, becomes the engine of adaptation. The pace of evolutionary change across all eukaryotes begins to quicken.
~750 -- 580 Ma
Snowball Earth
Possibly two episodes where Earth freezes entirely or nearly so -- ice sheets reaching the equator. Surviving microbes sheltered near hydrothermal vents. The melt that follows floods the ocean with nutrients and may have triggered the explosion of animal life that followed.
~580 Ma
Ediacaran Biota
The first complex multicellular organisms -- soft-bodied, frond-like, disc-shaped creatures covering the seafloor. Strange and alien, most have no clear relationship to any living animal group. Some may be the ancestors of cnidarians. Most vanish before the Cambrian with no known descendants.
538.8 +/- 0.6 Ma
Cambrian Explosion
Between 20 and 35 animal phyla appear within roughly 13--25 million years (Britannica). Eyes, shells, jointed legs, bilateral symmetry, predation -- all the body plans of every animal alive today emerge in this single geological instant. The Burgess Shale in Canada preserves soft-bodied Cambrian animals in extraordinary detail.
~475 Ma
Plants Colonise Land
Green algae make the transition to land. The first land plants -- relatives of modern liverworts -- begin transforming bare rock into soil. Within 100 million years, vast forests cover the continents. Atmospheric oxygen rises dramatically as forests spread, eventually reaching 35% in the Carboniferous.
~375 Ma
First Tetrapods
Fish with lobed fins evolve into the first four-limbed vertebrates -- tetrapods. Tiktaalik roseae, discovered in 2004 in Arctic Canada, is a transitional form with a neck, ribs, and proto-limbs. The colonisation of land by vertebrates begins. All amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals trace to this moment.
~320 Ma
Amniotes -- Reptiles and Mammals Diverge
The amniotic egg -- with its internal membrane protecting the embryo -- frees vertebrates entirely from dependence on water for reproduction. Reptiles and synapsids (the lineage leading to mammals) split. By 300 Ma, synapsids are the dominant land vertebrates. They will be dethroned by archosaurs after the End-Permian extinction.
252.2 -- 66 Ma
Mesozoic Era -- Age of Dinosaurs
The Triassic (252--201 Ma), Jurassic (201--145 Ma), and Cretaceous (145--66 Ma). After the End-Permian extinction destroys 96% of species, archosaurs rise to dominate. True dinosaurs appear ~235 Ma. By the Jurassic, sauropods like Argentinosaurus (up to 80 tonnes) and theropods like Allosaurus rule. The first mammals are small, nocturnal, and mostly insectivorous throughout.
~150 Ma
First Birds
Archaeopteryx lithographica -- part feathered theropod, part bird -- is found in 150 Ma Jurassic limestone in Germany. It has wings with claws, teeth, and a bony tail. True birds (Aves) diversify through the Cretaceous. When the asteroid hits at 66 Ma, the avian line is the only dinosaur lineage to survive.
~130 Ma
Flowering Plants Appear
Angiosperms -- flowering plants -- evolve and within 30 million years come to dominate plant life globally, co-evolving with insects (especially bees), birds, and later mammals for pollination and seed dispersal. Today they comprise ~295,000 species and are the foundation of almost every terrestrial food web.
66 Ma
Chicxulub Impact
A 10--20 km asteroid strikes the Yucatan Peninsula at ~72,000 km/h. The impact releases energy equivalent to 1 billion Hiroshima bombs. Global wildfires, years of nuclear winter, and ocean acidification follow. 76% of all species vanish. Every non-avian dinosaur is gone. Mammals and birds -- which survived in burrows and treetops -- begin their rapid radiation.
~55 Ma
Eocene Thermal Maximum -- Mammals Diversify
A rapid global warming event (PETM) drives explosive mammalian diversification. The first horses (Eohippus, dog-sized), early whales (Pakicetus, a land mammal entering water), early primates, and giant flightless birds all appear within a few million years of the asteroid impact.
~35 Ma
Antarctic Ice Sheet Forms
The Drake Passage opens between Antarctica and South America as Gondwana fragments. The resulting circumpolar current isolates Antarctica thermally. An ice sheet forms for the first time since the Permian. Global climate cools. Grasslands spread. Grazers evolve. The world begins to look recognisably modern.
8 -- 6 Ma
Human -- Chimpanzee Divergence
The last common ancestor of humans and chimpanzees lives in Africa. The exact split is debated -- genomic evidence points to 6--7 Ma; candidate fossils include Sahelanthropus tchadensis (~7 Ma, Chad) and Orrorin tugenensis (~6 Ma, Kenya). The lineage leading to all modern humans -- the hominin line -- begins here.
~4.4 Ma
Ardipithecus -- Early Bipedalism
Ardipithecus ramidus -- discovered in Ethiopia -- shows evidence of facultative bipedalism while retaining arboreal adaptations. Earlier than the famous "Lucy" (Australopithecus afarensis, ~3.2 Ma), Ardi forces a major rethink of human evolution's relationship to knuckle-walking.
~2.5 Ma
Genus Homo Appears -- Stone Tools
The first members of genus Homo (Homo habilis) and the oldest confirmed stone tools (Lomekwian, ~3.3 Ma; Oldowan, ~2.6 Ma) appear in Africa. Brain size begins a sustained increase. The Pleistocene ice ages begin -- glacial cycles of ~100,000 years will shape human evolution and geography for the next 2.5 million years.
~1.9 Ma
Homo erectus
The first hominin to leave Africa. Homo erectus spreads across Asia and possibly Europe by ~1.7 Ma. Uses fire (possibly). Cooks food -- a critical cognitive and physical development. Persists for ~1.8 million years, the longest-lived Homo species. Fossils found in Georgia, Java, and China.
~300,000 ya
Homo sapiens
Earliest Homo sapiens fossils from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco (~315,000 ya). Anatomically modern humans with high rounded skulls, flat faces, and chins. Other Homo species coexist: Neanderthals in Europe and western Asia, Denisovans in Siberia and Asia, Homo naledi in South Africa. Modern humans interbreed with Neanderthals -- most non-African humans carry 1--4% Neanderthal DNA.
~70,000 -- 50,000 ya
Behavioural Modernity -- Out of Africa
Cave art, personal ornaments, long-distance trade, complex tools, symbolic thought. The main dispersal of behaviourally modern Homo sapiens from Africa. By 45,000 ya Australia is reached. By 40,000 ya modern humans reach Europe, where they encounter and eventually replace Neanderthals. By 15,000 ya the Americas are populated via Beringia.
~11,700 ya
Holocene Begins
The Pleistocene ends. The last glacial period gives way to the warm, stable interglacial we still inhabit. Sea levels rise ~120 metres. Agriculture begins independently in the Fertile Crescent (~12,000 ya), China (~9,000 ya), and the Americas (~8,000 ya). Every human civilisation -- every city, nation, and technological achievement -- exists entirely within this narrow warm window.
~4,000 ya (~2000 BCE)
Last Woolly Mammoths
A dwarf island population of woolly mammoths (Mammuthus primigenius) survives on Wrangel Island, Siberia, while the Egyptian Middle Kingdom is in full flourish. They are the last survivors of the Pleistocene megafauna. Their sudden extinction remains scientifically unresolved -- inbreeding collapse was ruled out by genomic evidence (Dehasque et al., Cell, 2024). Something sudden ended them.

Major Plant Groups

🌸
Angiosperms -- Flowering Plants
~295,000 species -- 74% of all plants
The dominant plant group on Earth. Evolved ~130 Ma, co-evolving with insects and birds for pollination. Produce enclosed seeds within fruits. Include all grasses, oaks, palms, orchids, roses, and virtually every crop plant. Orchids alone account for ~28,000 species -- the largest plant family. Duckweed (Wolffia) is the smallest flowering plant at 0.1 mm.
Dominant Group
🌲
Gymnosperms -- Naked Seed Plants
~1,100 species -- Ancient Lineage
Conifers, cycads, ginkgo, and gnetales. Seeds are unenclosed -- "naked." The Wollemi pine (Wollemia nobilis) was known only from 200 Ma fossils until a living population was discovered in a single canyon in Australia in 1994. The coastal redwood (Sequoia sempervirens) reaches 115m -- the tallest living tree. The bristlecone pine (Pinus longaeva) lives over 5,000 years.
Tallest + Oldest Trees
🌿
Pteridophytes -- Ferns and Allies
~12,000 fern species + ~1,300 lycophytes
Among the oldest vascular plants -- ferns appeared ~360 Ma. Do not produce seeds; reproduce via spores. The tree ferns of New Zealand and Australia (Cyathea) reach 20m. Aquatic ferns (Salvinia, Azolla) are ecological engineers -- Azolla was used 49 Ma in the Arctic to cool the planet by fixing CO2 (the Azolla Event).
360 Ma Old Lineage
🪨
Bryophytes -- Mosses, Liverworts, Hornworts
~20,000 species -- First Land Plants
The first plants to colonise land (~475 Ma). No vascular tissue -- water moves by osmosis. Sphagnum moss (peat moss) alone covers ~3% of Earth's land surface and stores ~500 billion tonnes of carbon -- more than all living forests combined. Plays a critical role in northern hemisphere carbon sequestration. Can survive desiccation and revive when wetted.
500 Bt Carbon Stored
🌴
Trees -- 73,274 Species
~9,200 species still undiscovered
The IUCN Global Tree Assessment (Oct 2024) found 46,337 tree species threatened -- more than all threatened birds, mammals, reptiles and amphibians combined. The Amazon basin alone holds ~16,000 tree species. The African baobab (Adansonia digitata) stores up to 100,000 litres of water in its trunk. Trees communicate through mycorrhizal networks -- the "Wood Wide Web."
46,337 Threatened
🍄
Fungi -- The Hidden Kingdom
~155K described of ~2.5M estimated
Not plants -- the kingdom Fungi is more closely related to animals than to plants. Mushrooms, yeasts, moulds, truffles. Mycelial networks permeate every gram of healthy forest soil -- the Armillaria ostoyae in Oregon covers 965 ha (the largest known organism). ~90% of land plants depend on mycorrhizal fungi to absorb water and nutrients. Only ~0.4% of fungi species have been IUCN-assessed.
Largest Organism on Earth
🌊
Algae and Phytoplankton
~50% of Earth's atmospheric oxygen
Marine phytoplankton produce approximately 50% of Earth's atmospheric oxygen (NOAA) -- possibly up to 85% (Scripps). Prochlorococcus, the most abundant photosynthetic organism on Earth, is 0.6 micrometres and produces up to 20% of the biosphere's O2. Kelp forests off California and Chile are among the most productive ecosystems, growing up to 60 cm per day.
Invisible Oxygen Engine
🏜
Succulents and Xerophytes
Adapted to drought and extremes
Cacti (family Cactaceae -- ~1,750 species, almost all native to the Americas) store water in fleshy stems. The saguaro cactus lives 150--200 years and provides habitat for >100 bird species. Euphorbias in Africa independently evolved near-identical succulent forms -- convergent evolution. Some desert plants (resurrection plants, Selaginella lepidophylla) survive total desiccation for years.
Convergent Evolution
🪴
Carnivorous Plants
~800 species across 19 genera
Evolved independently at least 6 times in nutrient-poor habitats. Venus flytrap (Dionaea muscipula) closes in 100 milliseconds. Nepenthes pitcher plants hold up to 3.5 litres and have been documented trapping rats and lizards. Sundews (Drosera, 200+ species) use sticky mucilage tentacles. Bladderworts (Utricularia, 230+ species) use vacuum traps -- the fastest plant motion known.
Evolved 6 Times
🌾
Grasses -- Foundation of Civilisation
Family Poaceae -- ~12,000 species
Wheat, rice, maize, barley, sorghum, oats, sugarcane, and bamboo are all grasses. Together they supply ~50% of all human calories. Grasses evolved ~55 Ma and their spread ~25 Ma drove the evolution of horses, cattle, antelopes, elephants, and their predators. Bamboo (Bambusoideae) is the fastest-growing plant -- some species grow 91 cm per day.
50% of Human Calories
🌺
Orchids -- Most Diverse Plant Family
~28,000 species -- on every continent except Antarctica
The largest flowering plant family. Extraordinary pollination specialisations: some mimic female bees (Ophrys), others mimic female flies, wasps, and even fungi. The vanilla orchid (Vanilla planifolia) produces the world's most popular flavour. Ghost orchid (Epipogium aphyllum) has no chlorophyll -- entirely parasitic on fungi. ~28% of orchids are threatened.
28,000 Species
🌱
Mangroves and Seagrasses
Coastal Carbon Vaults
Mangrove forests cover ~150,000 km2 of tropical coastlines and store 3--5x more carbon per hectare than tropical rainforests ("blue carbon"). Nursery habitat for ~80% of tropical fish species. Seagrasses (72 species) cover ~300,000 km2 of seafloor and capture carbon 35x faster than tropical forests per unit area. Both are critically threatened by coastal development.
Blue Carbon Vaults

Photosynthesis -- The Equation That Built the World

6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6 O2
Carbon dioxide and water, powered by photons, become glucose and oxygen. Performed by every green plant, every alga, and every cyanobacterium. In 3.5 billion years it transformed a toxic reducing atmosphere into the oxygen-rich air that every animal depends on. The most consequential chemical reaction in the history of the biosphere.

Primates -- Our Family

Western Lowland Gorilla
Gorilla gorilla gorilla
Critically Endangered. Largest living primate -- up to 200kg. Congo Basin. Primarily herbivorous. Highly social, matriarchal. Sharing ~98.4% of DNA with humans. Poaching, habitat loss, and Ebola are primary threats. Wild population estimated at ~100,000 but declining.
Critically Endangered
Cross River Gorilla
Gorilla gorilla diehli
Critically Endangered. Rarest great ape -- approximately 250--300 individuals remaining in the Nigeria--Cameroon highlands. Highly fragmented populations. Extinction risk within decades without intervention.
~250--300 Left
Sumatran Orangutan
Pongo abelii
Critically Endangered. Most arboreal of all great apes. Builds sleeping nests daily. Tool use -- uses sticks to extract insects and seeds. 14% of all orangutan habitat was destroyed 2000--2012. Palm oil expansion is the primary driver of forest loss.
Critically Endangered
Bornean Orangutan
Pongo pygmaeus
Endangered. Three subspecies. Population has declined by more than 50% over the past 60 years. Borneo has lost ~40% of its forest since 1950. Males develop large cheek pads (flanges) and long throat pouches for long-call communication.
Endangered
Chimpanzee
Pan troglodytes
Endangered. Four subspecies across equatorial Africa. Tool use, cooperative hunting, social learning, and rudimentary culture. Use stones to crack nuts, sticks to fish for termites. Share ~98.7% DNA with humans. Vocalisations include regional dialects.
Endangered
Bonobo
Pan paniscus
Endangered. Found only south of the Congo River, DRC. Society is female-dominant -- unusual among great apes. Known for using social and sexual behaviour to resolve conflict. Highly empathetic -- bonobos share food even with strangers. Population estimated at 10,000--50,000.
Endangered
Gelada
Theropithecus gelada
Least Concern but endemic and unique. "Bleeding heart" baboon of the Ethiopian highlands. The only primate that feeds primarily on grass. Lives at 1,800--4,400m altitude. Distinctive bare red chest patch used for social signalling. Forms bands of up to 1,200 individuals.
Ethiopian Endemic
Aye-aye
Daubentonia madagascariensis
Endangered. The strangest primate. An elongated middle finger with a specialised ball-and-socket joint is used for percussive foraging -- tapping on wood to locate hollow chambers, then extracting larvae. Nocturnal, solitary. Madagascar endemic. Considered an omen of death in local Malagasy tradition.
Endangered
Proboscis Monkey
Nasalis larvatus
Endangered. Borneo endemic. Males develop an enormous pendulous nose -- up to 10 cm -- used to amplify vocalisations and attract females. Highly specialised gut ferments leaves and neutralises toxins -- cannot digest ripe fruit. Excellent swimmers; cross rivers to avoid predators.
Endangered

Megafauna -- The Great Beasts

🦁
African Lion
Panthera leo -- Vulnerable
Approximately 20,000 lions remain in sub-Saharan Africa -- down from ~200,000 a century ago. The West African lion (P. l. leo) is Critically Endangered, with fewer than 400 individuals in fragmented populations. Lions are the only truly social big cat -- prides of 3--30. Males hold territories for 2--4 years on average before being displaced.
~20,000 Remaining
🐘
African Savanna Elephant
Loxodonta africana -- Vulnerable
Largest land animal. Up to 7 tonnes. Matriarchal herds led by elder females who memorise water sources and migration routes spanning hundreds of km. Mourn their dead. Self-medicate with plants. Communicate via infrasound across 10+ km. ~415,000 remaining -- declined by ~111,000 in the decade 2006--2016 (ivory poaching). Classified as separate species from Forest Elephant (LC to EN) since 2021.
Vulnerable
🦏
Northern White Rhino
Ceratotherium simum cottoni -- Functionally Extinct
Two females remain -- Najin and Fatu -- in Ol Pejeta Conservancy, Kenya. The last male, Sudan, died in 2018. IVF using stored sperm and eggs is the only hope for the subspecies. Southern White Rhino (NT, ~20,000) pulled back from 50 individuals in the 1890s -- one of conservation's greatest successes.
2 Left
🐆
Amur Leopard
Panthera pardus orientalis -- Critically Endangered
The world's rarest big cat. ~120 individuals in the wild -- Russian Far East and northeast China. Capable of running 58 km/h and leaping 6m horizontally. Keeps its kills cached in trees for up to a week. Conservation breeding and anti-poaching have slowly increased numbers from ~30 in 2007.
~120 Individuals
🐼
Giant Panda
Ailuropoda melanoleuca -- Vulnerable
~1,800 in the wild in the mountain forests of Sichuan, Shaanxi, and Gansu, China. Classified as Vulnerable in 2016, downgraded from Endangered -- a conservation success driven by habitat protection and captive breeding. Eats 12--38 kg of bamboo daily. Despite a carnivore's digestive system, bamboo comprises 99% of diet.
Vulnerable -- Recovering
🐯
Tiger
Panthera tigris -- Endangered
Fewer than 4,500 in the wild -- across 6 remaining subspecies. Bengal (EN, ~3,000), Indochinese (EN), Sumatran (CR, ~400), Siberian/Amur (EN, ~600), South China (possibly extinct in wild), Malayan (CR). Range has contracted by ~93% from historical extent. India holds ~70% of remaining wild tigers.
Endangered

Reptiles and Amphibians

🐊
Saltwater Crocodile
Crocodylus porosus -- Least Concern
The largest living reptile -- up to 6.3m, 1,000 kg. Can live 70+ years. Survived all five mass extinctions. Crocodilians have been evolutionarily stable for ~85 million years -- a design so functional that natural selection barely improved it. Capable of "death roll" torque force exceeding any other animal bite.
85 Ma Unchanged
🦎
Komodo Dragon
Varanus komodoensis -- Endangered
The largest living lizard -- up to 3m, 70 kg. Endemic to 5 Indonesian islands. Hunts using forked tongue to detect carrion and prey 9 km away. Venom glands in lower jaw prevent blood clotting. Can reproduce by parthenogenesis -- females can produce offspring without fertilisation. ~1,400 left in the wild.
~1,400 Left
🐢
Leatherback Sea Turtle
Dermochelys coriacea -- Vulnerable
The largest living turtle -- up to 2m, 900 kg. Dives to 1,280m -- deeper than any other reptile. Survives on jellyfish alone; lacks a hard shell (has a cartilaginous carapace). Migrates up to 20,000 km annually. Nesting beaches have declined catastrophically -- Pacific population fell 90% between 1980--2000.
Vulnerable
🐸
Panamanian Golden Frog
Atelopus zeteki -- Critically Endangered
Possibly extinct in the wild. The chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis, Bd) has devastated amphibian populations globally -- the most catastrophic infectious disease ever recorded in vertebrates. Bd has caused population declines in at least 500 amphibian species and driven 90 species to extinction. ~41% of all amphibian species are now threatened.
Possibly Wild Extinct
🦕
Tuatara
Sphenodon punctatus -- Least Concern (NZ)
The only surviving member of the order Rhynchocephalia -- a lineage that has persisted for 250 million years, separating from lizards and snakes before the dinosaurs rose. A "living fossil" in the strictest sense. Third eye on top of skull (pineal eye). Lives 100+ years. Found only in New Zealand, reintroduced to mainland after mammalian predator eradication.
250 Ma Lineage
🐍
Green Anaconda
Eunectes murinus -- Least Concern
The heaviest snake -- up to 250 kg, 9m. Hunts by constriction in Amazonian flooded forests. Not venomous. Ambushes prey including capybaras, deer, caimans, and occasionally jaguars. Females are significantly larger than males -- extreme sexual dimorphism. Males engage in "breeding balls" involving up to 13 males competing for a single female over weeks.
Heaviest Snake

Birds -- 11,017 Living Dinosaurs

Wandering Albatross
Diomedea exulans
Largest wingspan of any living bird -- up to 3.7m. Circumnavigates the Southern Ocean, covering 120,000 km/year. Can glide for hours without a wingbeat using dynamic soaring. Mates for life (50+ years). Breeds on sub-Antarctic islands every 2 years. Critically threatened by longline fishing bycatch.
Vulnerable
Kakapo
Strigops habroptilus
Critically Endangered. The world's heaviest parrot (up to 4 kg) and the only flightless parrot. Nocturnal. Incredibly long-lived (90+ years). New Zealand. Only 247 individuals alive (2024). Each bird is named and individually managed. Only breeds when rimu trees fruit -- every 2--5 years. A remarkable conservation recovery from 51 individuals in 1995.
247 Individuals
Emperor Penguin
Aptenodytes forsteri
Tallest and heaviest penguin -- up to 1.2m, 46 kg. Breeds on Antarctic sea ice in -60 degrees C. Males incubate a single egg on their feet for 65 days, fasting throughout, huddling in rotating formations to maintain warmth. Dives to 565m -- deeper than any other bird. Sea ice loss threatens 90%+ of colonies by 2100.
Vulnerable
California Condor
Gymnogyps californianus
Critically Endangered. Largest land bird in North America -- 2.9m wingspan. Extinct in the wild by 1987 (22 individuals remained). Captive breeding and reintroduction have brought numbers to ~500. Lead poisoning from ammunition in carcasses remains the primary ongoing threat. Can live 60+ years.
~500 -- Back from 22
African Grey Parrot
Psittacus erithacus
Endangered. Considered the most intelligent bird -- demonstrates understanding of object permanence, zero concept, and statistical inference. Alex, a research bird studied by Dr Irene Pepperberg for 30 years, had a functional vocabulary of 150+ words with comprehension. Wild populations devastated by the illegal pet trade.
Endangered
Sword-billed Hummingbird
Ensifera ensifera
The only bird with a bill longer than its body. Bill reaches 10.2 cm -- evolved to access nectar of Passiflora mixta and Datura flowers with correspondingly long corollas. A textbook example of co-evolution: the plant and bird are mutually dependent. Andes of South America, 1,700--3,500m altitude.
Extreme Co-evolution

Insects -- The Dominant Animals

🐝
Bees
~20,000 species -- Critical Pollinators
Honey bees (Apis mellifera) pollinate crops worth ~$577 billion annually. The blue orchard mason bee is 120x more efficient than honey bees for apple pollination. Bumblebees (250 species) use "buzz pollination" -- sonication -- to extract pollen from tomato flowers. North American bee populations have declined ~25% since 1990. Colony Collapse Disorder has destroyed ~30% of US honey bee colonies annually since 2006.
25% Decline
🦋
Monarch Butterfly
Danaus plexippus -- Endangered
Annual migration of up to 4,000 km between Canada/US and Mexican overwintering forests -- the longest insect migration known. How they navigate is still not fully understood; involves a sun compass and possibly magnetic sensing. Population at Mexican overwintering sites declined ~80% over 20 years. Milkweed loss and climate change are primary drivers.
80% Decline
🐜
Ants
~20,000 species -- Ecological Engineers
By biomass, ants may represent ~20% of all animal biomass on land. Move more soil than earthworms in many ecosystems. Leafcutter ants cultivate fungi -- the world's oldest agriculture, predating human farming by 50 million years. Army ants (Eciton) form living bridges, bivouacs, and raid fronts of 200,000 workers. Supercolonies of the Argentine ant span continents.
20% of Land Animal Biomass
🪲
Beetles
~400,000 species -- Most Diverse Order
When J.B.S. Haldane was asked what can be inferred about God from creation, he reputedly said "an inordinate fondness for beetles." One in four described animal species is a beetle (Order Coleoptera). Occur in every habitat except open ocean and polar ice. Dung beetles (Scarabaeidae) navigate using the Milky Way -- the first animal proven to use the galaxy for orientation.

Ocean -- The Deep Unknown

The ocean covers 71% of Earth. We have better maps of Mars than of the seabed. Only 0.001% of the deep ocean floor has ever been seen by human eyes. It is the last great wilderness -- and it is dying faster than we can document it.

Ocean Depth Zones

  • Sunlight Zone
    0 -- 200m
    Epipelagic zone. Receives sunlight -- site of all photosynthesis. Most ocean life is concentrated here. Coral reefs, kelp forests, sea turtles, dolphins, flying fish, tuna.
  • Twilight Zone
    200 -- 1,000m
    Mesopelagic. Dim light, no photosynthesis. Many animals migrate vertically -- feeding in the sunlight zone at night, hiding in the dark by day. Lanternfish, hatchetfish, siphonophores. One of Earth's largest fish biomass layers.
  • Midnight Zone
    1,000 -- 4,000m
    Bathypelagic. Complete darkness. Bioluminescence is the only light. Anglerfish, sperm whales (diving), deep-sea squid, vampire squid. Water temperature 4 degrees C. Pressure 100--400 atmospheres.
  • Abyssal Zone
    4,000 -- 6,000m
    Abyssopelagic. Covers ~60% of Earth's surface -- the largest habitat on the planet. Near-freezing water, crushing pressure, complete darkness, slow "marine snow" of organic particles drifting down. Sea cucumbers, brittle stars, polychaete worms, foraminifera.
  • Hadal Zone
    6,000 -- 11,034m
    Ocean trenches only. The Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench (10,935m) is the deepest known point -- if Everest were placed there it would be 2km underwater. Life exists even here: amphipod crustaceans, polychaetes, foraminifera. Plastic has been found at every depth sampled.

Remarkable Ocean Species

Blue Whale
Balaenoptera musculus
The largest animal ever to have existed -- up to 33m, 190 tonnes. Heart the size of a small car. Calls reaching 188 decibels -- the loudest of any animal. Eat 4 tonnes of krill daily in summer. Endangered. Numbers ~10,000--25,000 (rebuilt from ~360 in 1960s following commercial whaling).
Largest Animal Ever
Sperm Whale
Physeter macrocephalus
Largest toothed predator. Dives to 3,000m, holding breath for 90 minutes, to hunt giant squid. Has the largest brain of any animal. Clicks (codas) are complex social communication -- Project CETI is using AI to decode them. Carries a waxy oil called spermaceti in a massive head cavity -- possibly for echolocation focusing.
Largest Brain
Giant Pacific Octopus
Enteroctopus dofleini
The world's largest octopus -- arm span up to 9m. Three hearts, blue blood (haemocyanin). Two-thirds of neurons in their arms, not their brain. Can change colour, texture, and pattern in 0.3 seconds. Opens jars, solves multi-step puzzles, plays. Lives only 3--5 years despite remarkable intelligence.
Distributed Intelligence
Whale Shark
Rhincodon typus
Largest fish -- up to 18m, 19 tonnes. Feeds by filter-feeding -- engulfing plankton, fish eggs, and small fish. Has 3,000 tiny teeth (vestigial) and eyes with photoreceptors on its skin. Endangered -- slow reproduction (maturity at 25--30 years). The world's largest known aggregation (~400 individuals) occurs off Yucatan during whale shark season.
Largest Fish
Mantis Shrimp
Stomatopoda (order)
Sight unlike any other animal -- 16 types of photoreceptors (humans have 3). Can detect UV, infrared, and circular polarised light. Club-type mantis shrimps strike at 23m/s -- 10,000x the acceleration of a bullet -- generating cavitation bubbles that implode with sufficient force to stun prey. Strike force comparable to a .22 calibre bullet.
16-Color Vision
Mimic Octopus
Thaumoctopus mimicus
The only known animal that can dynamically mimic multiple different species. Impersonates lionfish, flatfish, and sea snakes -- choosing based on context. Discovered only in 1998 off Sulawesi, Indonesia. Demonstrates higher-order cognitive flexibility: not a fixed response but deliberate selection of the most appropriate mimic for the current predator.
Discovered 1998
Coral
Order Scleractinia (stone corals)
~1,600 reef-building coral species. Each coral polyp is a tiny animal -- related to jellyfish. Lives in symbiosis with photosynthetic algae (zooxanthellae) that provide 90% of the coral's energy. When sea temperature rises by 1 degree C for extended periods, corals expel their algae ("bleaching") and die. 84% of the world's reefs bleached between Jan 2023 and Mar 2025 (ICRI/NOAA).
84% Bleached 2023--25
Immortal Jellyfish
Turritopsis dohrnii
The only known biologically immortal animal. When stressed or aged, it reverts to its juvenile polyp state -- transdifferentiation. Can repeat the cycle indefinitely. Discovered in the 1990s. Now globally distributed via ballast water. Does not mean it cannot die -- predation, disease, and injury still kill it -- but it has no intrinsic lifespan limit.
Biologically Immortal
Hydrothermal Vent Life
Multiple phyla
Discovered in 1977 by the Alvin submersible at the Galapagos Rift. Entire ecosystems powered by chemosynthesis -- no sunlight required. Giant tube worms (Riftia pachyptila) up to 3m, growing the fastest of any marine invertebrate. Vent shrimp with light-sensitive patches on their backs to detect the faint glow of vent emissions. Life beyond photosynthesis.
No Sunlight Needed

Earth Systems Science

Systems -- How Earth Breathes

The invisible machinery. Oxygen, water, carbon, nitrogen -- cycling endlessly through rock, air, ocean, and every living cell. Disrupt one cycle and every other shifts in response.

O2
Oxygen Cycle
~50% of atmospheric oxygen is produced by marine phytoplankton (NOAA). Prochlorococcus, the smallest photosynthesizer, accounts for up to 20% alone. Terrestrial plants produce the remainder. Important clarification: the Amazon's gross O2 production is large, but its decomposition and respiration consume an equal amount -- the net contribution to atmospheric O2 is approximately zero (NASA/FactCheck.org). The O2 we breathe is primarily a product of ocean phytoplankton.
H2O
Water Cycle (Hydrological Cycle)
Solar energy evaporates water from oceans (~86% of total evaporation), lakes, rivers, and land. Plants transpire enormous quantities -- a single large oak releases 400 litres per day. Water vapour rises, cools, condenses into clouds, and precipitates as rain, snow, or hail. Runoff feeds rivers; infiltration feeds groundwater aquifers. The same water molecules have cycled through this system for 4 billion years.
C
Carbon Cycle
Carbon moves between four reservoirs: atmosphere, biosphere (living organisms), hydrosphere (ocean absorbs ~25% of human CO2 emissions), and lithosphere (fossil fuels, limestone). Amazon trees store ~100 billion tonnes. Soils store ~3x more carbon than the atmosphere. Since 1750, atmospheric CO2 has risen from 280 ppm to ~425 ppm (2026) -- a 52% increase and the fastest sustained increase in at least 800,000 years of ice-core records.
N
Nitrogen Cycle
78% of air is N2, but most organisms cannot use it directly. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium in legume root nodules; free-living Azotobacter) convert N2 to ammonia. Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonia to nitrates -- the form plants absorb. Denitrifying bacteria return N2 to the atmosphere. Human application of synthetic nitrogen fertiliser (Haber-Bosch process) has doubled the amount of biologically available nitrogen on Earth, with profound ecological consequences.
P
Phosphorus Cycle
Unlike N and C, phosphorus has no significant atmospheric phase -- it cycles only through rock, soil, water, and organisms. Weathering of phosphate rock releases phosphorus into soil; plants absorb it; animals eat plants; organisms die and decompose; phosphorus returns to soil or washes to the ocean and ultimately settles as sediment. A slow cycle -- millions of years for deep ocean phosphorus to return to land. Often the limiting nutrient in freshwater ecosystems.
Sol
Solar Energy and Food Webs
Solar energy enters the biosphere through photosynthesis. Only ~1--3% of incident solar energy is captured. Each trophic level transfers ~10% of energy to the next (Lindeman's 10% rule) -- explaining why food chains rarely exceed 5 levels and why carnivores are always rarer than herbivores. Decomposers (fungi, bacteria) return nutrients from dead organic matter to the soil, completing the cycle.
Soil
Soil -- The Living Foundation
A single teaspoon of healthy forest soil contains more living organisms than there are people on Earth: ~10,000 species of bacteria, 5,000 fungi, 1,000 protozoa. Topsoil takes ~500 years per 2.5 cm to form. ~33% of Earth's soil is moderately to highly degraded (FAO 2015). Soil organic carbon stores 2--3x more carbon than the atmosphere. Without soil microbial activity, virtually no land ecosystem functions.
Ice
Cryosphere
Ice sheets (Antarctica: 26.5M km3; Greenland: 2.85M km3), glaciers, permafrost, and sea ice. Antarctica's ice sheet contains ~70% of Earth's fresh water -- if melted, sea level would rise ~58m. Arctic sea ice has declined ~13% per decade since 1979 (satellite records). Permafrost contains ~1.5 trillion tonnes of frozen organic carbon -- twice the current atmospheric carbon. Thawing permafrost is a major positive feedback risk.

Biomes of Earth

Tropical Rainforest
~6% of land -- ~50% of all species
Amazon (5.5M km2), Congo (3.3M km2), Southeast Asian. 25--40 metres of precipitation annually. The Amazon alone holds ~16,000 tree species and 3 million species overall. Canopy layers create multiple microhabitats. Primary forest cannot be replanted -- the mycorrhizal complexity of old-growth forest takes centuries to develop.
Boreal Forest (Taiga)
Largest land biome -- 17M km2
Circumpolar conifer belt across Russia (Siberian taiga), Canada, and Scandinavia. Stores ~30% of all terrestrial carbon. Dominated by spruce, pine, fir, and larch. Long cold winters; short growing seasons. Wolf, brown bear, lynx, moose, wolverine. Rapidly warming -- fires and permafrost thaw are releasing sequestered carbon.
Grasslands and Savannas
~40% of ice-free land surface
African savanna, South American cerrado, North American prairie, Eurasian steppe. Support the world's largest terrestrial migrations -- wildebeest (1.3 million), Mongolian gazelle. Deep root systems store carbon below ground -- grasslands may be more resilient carbon sinks than forests under climate change. 49% of grasslands have been converted to cropland globally.
Desert and Xeric Shrubland
~33% of land surface
The Sahara (9M km2), Arabian Desert, Gobi, Atacama. Not lifeless -- the Namib Desert has been continuously arid for 55 million years and hosts extraordinary endemic life (Welwitschia lives 1,000--2,000 years). The driest place on Earth, the Atacama, received measurable rain in 2015 for the first time in recorded history -- and dormant flower seeds erupted within days.
Wetlands
~9M km2 -- 40% of all species dependent
Swamps, marshes, bogs, mangroves, estuaries, floodplains. Cover ~6% of land but support ~40% of all species and provide ecosystem services worth $47 trillion annually (more per hectare than any other biome). Absorb 5x more carbon per hectare than tropical rainforest. 35% of natural wetlands lost since 1970 -- faster than any other ecosystem.
Freshwater Systems
~2.5% of all water -- 10% of all species
Rivers, lakes, streams, and groundwater contain only 2.5% of Earth's water, yet support ~10% of all known species including ~35,000 fish species. The Amazon River discharges 20% of all river water entering Earth's oceans. The Congo River is the world's deepest (220m) and has the highest discharge of any river in Africa. Lake Baikal holds 20% of all surface freshwater on Earth.

Events -- When Earth Strikes Back

The Five Mass Extinctions

  • End-Ordovician
    ~445 Ma
    ~85% marine species

  • Late Devonian
    ~372 Ma
    ~75% of species

  • End-Permian "Great Dying"
    ~252 Ma

  • End-Triassic
    ~201 Ma
    ~70--80% of species

  • End-Cretaceous (K-Pg)
    66 Ma
    76% of all species
    Chicxulub asteroid impact (10--20 km, 120-mile crater, Yucatan) plus coincident Deccan Traps volcanism; nuclear winter lasting years; ocean acidification

Earth Events -- Forces of Nature

🌋
Volcanoes
~1,350 potentially active volcanoes worldwide. 50--70 erupt each year. The Siberian Traps (End-Permian, ~252 Ma) erupted continuously for ~1 million years, releasing enough CO2 and SO2 to acidify the ocean and heat the atmosphere by 10 degrees C. Krakatoa (1883) killed 36,000 people and lowered global temperatures by 1.2 degrees C for 5 years. Yellowstone's last supereruption (~631,000 ya) deposited ash across half of North America.
Tambora 1815: coldest year in recorded history followed
🌊
Tsunamis
Generated by undersea earthquakes, submarine landslides, or volcanic collapse. The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami (Mw 9.1--9.3) killed 230,000+ people across 14 countries -- the deadliest natural disaster of the 21st century. Generated by a 1,200 km fault rupture. Waves reached 30m in Aceh, Indonesia. Travel across entire oceans at 800 km/h -- jet aircraft speed -- in deep water.
2004: 230,000+ dead across 14 countries
🌏
Earthquakes
~500,000 detectable earthquakes annually. ~100 are felt. ~20 cause major damage. The 1960 Valdivia earthquake (Mw 9.5, Chile) remains the most powerful ever recorded. The Earth's tectonic system has opened every ocean, built every mountain range, and driven the evolutionary isolation that created much of Earth's biodiversity. Every continent is moving -- at the speed fingernails grow.
1960 Valdivia: Mw 9.5 -- strongest recorded
🔥
Wildfires
Australia's 2019--2020 bushfires burned ~73,000 sq mi -- an area larger than Syria -- and affected approximately 3 billion animals (WWF). Canada's 2023 wildfires burned 18 million hectares -- the worst in recorded history, releasing more CO2 in a single season than Canada typically emits in a decade. Climate change is extending fire seasons on every forested continent and increasing the frequency of "megafires."
~3 billion animals affected -- Australia 2020
🌀
Hurricanes and Cyclones
Tropical cyclones draw energy from warm ocean surface water. ~80--90 named tropical storms form annually. Category 5 storms have been intensifying -- warmer oceans provide more energy. Hurricane Patricia (2015, Eastern Pacific) reached 345 km/h winds -- the strongest ever recorded. Storm surges are the primary killer; a 1970 Bangladesh cyclone killed ~500,000 in a single event.
Patricia 2015: 345 km/h -- strongest ever
🌊
Floods
The deadliest natural disaster category historically. The 1931 China floods (Huai and Yellow rivers) killed 1--4 million people. The 2022 Pakistan floods submerged one-third of the country, affecting 33 million people. Google Flood Hub now provides 7-day advance warning in 80+ countries. Extreme precipitation events are increasing in frequency as a warmer atmosphere holds 7% more water per degree C of warming (Clausius-Clapeyron).
2022 Pakistan: one-third of country submerged
❄
Ice Ages and Glaciations
Earth has experienced at least 5 major glacial periods in its history. The Pleistocene (2.6 Ma -- 11,700 ya) featured ~50 glacial cycles. At the Last Glacial Maximum (~20,000 ya), ice sheets 3--4 km thick covered northern Europe and North America. Sea levels were 120m lower -- Britain was connected to Europe; Bering Land Bridge linked Asia to America. We are in an interglacial now. Without human CO2 emissions, the next glacial cycle would begin in ~50,000 years.
Last Maximum: sea level 120m lower
☄
Asteroid Impacts
The Chicxulub impactor (66 Ma) was 10--20 km wide and released energy equivalent to 1 billion nuclear bombs. The crater (180 km diameter, Yucatan) is still visible. Earth has ~190 confirmed impact craters. The Chelyabinsk meteor (2013, Russia) was 20m, released 30x the energy of Hiroshima, and injured 1,500 people from shockwave glass. Near-Earth object tracking detects ~3 new potentially hazardous asteroids weekly.
Chicxulub: 1 billion nuclear bombs equivalent

The Sixth Extinction -- Now

A geological mass extinction requires the loss of 75% or more of species. We are not there yet. But the current rate of species loss is 100 to 1,000 times the natural background rate (Dasgupta 2021) -- the fastest sustained acceleration in the fossil record outside the five canonical events. The cause is unambiguous: habitat destruction, overexploitation, pollution, invasive species, and climate change, all driven by one species.

The WWF Living Planet Report 2024, based on 35,000 population trends across 5,495 species, documents a 73% average decline in monitored wildlife populations between 1970 and 2020. This is not yet extinction -- it is the draining of life before extinction. The signal is clear.

The ocean is acidifying at a rate 10 times faster than at any point in the past 66 million years. Insect populations in some monitored regions have declined by 75% in less than 30 years (Krefeld entomological study, Germany, 2017). Freshwater vertebrates have declined by an average of 83% since 1970. The Amazon is approaching a tipping point beyond which it may transition from rainforest to savanna, releasing its stored carbon and accelerating the very change driving it.

Note: The "Sixth Extinction" designation is contested among scientists. The case rests on extinction rate, not yet magnitude. The background geological extinction rate is approximately 0.1 species per million species-years. Current observed rates are orders of magnitude higher. Whether this constitutes a mass extinction event by geological standards depends on timescale -- but on any human timescale, it is catastrophic.

The Long Horizon -- What AI Could Become for Nature
NVIDIA Earth-2 is the first serious attempt at a real-time digital twin of Earth's climate system. Project CETI, if successful, would mark the first time a non-human communication system was decoded at semantic depth. The convergence of ubiquitous environmental sensors, satellite constellations, large AI models, and real-time data pipelines makes a new kind of intelligence imaginable: distributed AI guardians monitoring every major biome, detecting tipping points before they tip, and translating the state of the Earth into language both humans and machines can act on. Not hypothetical. In construction. The infrastructure exists. The question is whether it is deployed fast enough.
